Vietnam’s Logistics Workforce: Abundant Talent, but a Shortage of High-Quality Professionals

Vietnam’s logistics industry continues to expand rapidly, driven by the growth of manufacturing, international trade, e-commerce, and foreign direct investment (FDI).

As a result, demand for professionals in logistics, warehousing, import-export operations, freight forwarding, and supply chain management continues to increase.

However, companies are facing a significant recruitment challenge:

While the overall supply of logistics candidates is relatively large, there remains a shortage of high-quality professionals who combine strong technical expertise, practical experience, foreign-language skills, and management capabilities.

For Japanese and other foreign-invested companies operating in Vietnam, there is a considerable difference between “receiving applications” and “finding the right talent.”

1. Vietnam Has a Growing Supply of Logistics Talent

As Vietnam’s logistics sector develops, more universities and educational institutions are offering programs in Logistics and Supply Chain Management, International Business, Import-Export, Transportation, and International Trade.

At the same time, the number of experienced professionals working for freight forwarders, shipping lines, transportation companies, warehouses, manufacturers, and import-export companies has also increased.

For entry-level and general staff positions, companies may therefore receive a reasonable number of applications.

The real challenge begins when companies need to recruit experienced specialists, supervisors, managers, and other highly skilled professionals.

2. Why Is There Still a Shortage of High-Quality Logistics Professionals?

Modern logistics requires much more than knowledge of shipping documents or import-export procedures.

Companies increasingly need professionals who understand the entire supply chain and can coordinate effectively across multiple functions.

Key capabilities include:

  • Strong knowledge of Logistics and Supply Chain Management;
  • Practical experience in import-export, warehousing, transportation, and freight forwarding;
  • Supplier and logistics service provider management;
  • Logistics cost analysis and optimization;
  • Inventory, lead-time, and supply planning management;
  • Experience with ERP, WMS, and TMS systems;
  • Strong Japanese and/or English communication skills;
  • Team management and cross-functional coordination;
  • Experience working in Japanese or multinational companies.

Candidates who meet several of these requirements simultaneously are relatively limited.

This explains why a company may receive dozens of CVs but still struggle to identify a candidate who truly matches its requirements.

3. What Kind of Logistics Talent Do Japanese Companies in Vietnam Need?

For Japanese companies, professional expertise alone is often not enough.

Candidates are also expected to work systematically, follow established procedures, provide accurate reports, manage schedules and deadlines, solve problems effectively, and communicate smoothly with Japanese managers.

Professionals who combine Logistics/Supply Chain expertise + management experience + Japanese-language ability are particularly valuable.

For example, a Logistics Manager who understands factory operations in Vietnam, manages local suppliers, freight forwarders and transportation companies, and can report directly to Japanese management or headquarters can provide significantly greater value than an employee focused only on individual logistics tasks.

4. Logistics and Supply Chain Positions Becoming More Difficult to Recruit

Recruitment demand is no longer limited to import-export staff. Companies increasingly need experienced specialists and management-level professionals, including:

Logistics Manager / Logistics Supervisor
Responsible for logistics operations, transportation, warehousing, and logistics cost management.

Supply Chain Manager
Oversees purchasing, planning, inventory, logistics, and delivery across the supply chain.

Warehouse Manager
Manages warehouse operations, personnel, inventory accuracy, productivity, and warehouse management systems.

Purchasing / Procurement Manager
Responsible for purchasing activities, procurement strategy, supplier development, and supplier management.

Import–Export Manager
Manages international trade operations, customs clearance, documentation, and regulatory compliance.

SCM Planning / Production Planning
Responsible for production planning, material requirements, inventory levels, and supply-demand coordination.

Japanese-speaking Logistics Professionals
Support communication with Japanese managers, headquarters, customers, and business partners.

At the management level, many strong candidates are already employed and are not actively searching for new opportunities.

As a result, traditional job advertising alone may not be sufficient to reach the best candidates.

5. The Real Challenge Is Not “Finding CVs” but “Finding the Right People”

In logistics recruitment, the number of applications does not necessarily determine recruitment success.

A company may receive 50 CVs, but if the candidates do not meet its requirements in terms of industry experience, technical knowledge, foreign-language skills, management capabilities, or cultural fit, the recruitment problem remains unresolved.

For high-quality and management-level recruitment, companies therefore need to move from:

“Post a vacancy and wait for applications”

to:

“Identify the right candidates and proactively approach them.”

This is why headhunting and proactive recruitment are increasingly effective for Logistics, Supply Chain, and management positions.
