How Is the Workforce Structure of Japanese Companies in Vietnam Changing?

From traditional operational roles to specialized talent, local management, and professionals bridging Japan and Vietnam

In recent years, Vietnam's labor market has witnessed significant changes in the recruitment needs of Japanese companies. In addition to production workers, administrative staff, and traditional operational personnel, companies increasingly need engineers, IT professionals, business development specialists, middle managers, and employees capable of working directly with their headquarters in Japan.

These changes are driven not only by digital transformation and business expansion, but also by intensifying competition for talent, the need to improve productivity, and the growing importance of strengthening local management capabilities.

1. Japanese Companies Continue to Expand, but Recruitment Is Becoming More Challenging

According to the Japan External Trade Organization (JETRO)'s FY2025 Survey on Business Conditions of Japanese Companies Operating Overseas, conducted from August 19 to September 17, 2025, with responses from 906 Japanese companies operating in Vietnam:

  • 56.9% planned to expand their business operations over the next one to two years, ranking Vietnam first in ASEAN for the second consecutive year.

  • 67.5% expected to be profitable in 2025, the highest level since 2009.

  • 48.2% reported that recruitment had become more difficult over the preceding two years.

Source: JETRO, FY2025 Survey on Business Conditions of Japanese Companies Operating Overseas and its Vietnam market analysis, February 2026. 

These figures highlight a key challenge: although business growth opportunities remain strong, the ability to recruit the right people is becoming an increasingly important factor in determining whether companies can successfully expand.

Recruitment challenges are particularly pronounced in manufacturing. According to JETRO's analysis, 76.0% of manufacturing companies in northern Vietnam reported recruitment difficulties, compared with 56.7% in southern Vietnam. Competition for talent from Chinese, Taiwanese, and South Korean companies was among the factors discussed in the report.

Source: JETRO, Vietnam market analysis, February 13, 2026.

2. Four Key Workforce Trends Among Japanese Companies in Vietnam

Trend 1: From Operational Personnel to Specialized and Value-Creating Talent

Traditionally, many Japanese companies in Vietnam have focused on building workforces for manufacturing, quality control, administration, and operational functions aligned with the processes and standards of their Japanese headquarters.

Today, as companies seek to improve productivity and develop their businesses, recruitment demand is expanding toward professionals with specialized expertise, problem-solving capabilities, and a continuous improvement mindset.

Key positions include:

  • Software engineers, Bridge System Engineers (BrSEs), IT Project Managers, and IT Consultants.

  • Manufacturing engineers, automation specialists, quality management professionals, and process improvement specialists.

  • Supply chain, logistics, and procurement professionals.

  • Business development specialists, B2B sales professionals, and corporate account managers.

This does not mean that companies are abandoning operational recruitment. Rather, the range of capabilities required within the workforce is becoming more diverse. Beyond executing established procedures, employees are increasingly expected to analyze problems, improve processes, and contribute directly to business performance.

Trend 2: A Growing Role for Vietnamese Managers

As Japanese companies expand their operations in Vietnam, they need local managers who can oversee daily operations, manage employees, control costs, and coordinate effectively with departments in Japan.

Consequently, positions such as Department Manager, Operations Manager, Sales Manager, HR Manager, and Project Manager are becoming increasingly relevant to workforce planning.

Vietnamese professionals with experience in Japanese business environments, including familiarity with reporting procedures, quality management, and Japanese corporate culture, can offer a significant advantage.

Developing local management talent can also reduce reliance on personnel transfers from Japan while improving responsiveness to local markets and customers.

This should be understood as a strategic organizational development opportunity rather than an assumption that all Japanese companies are replacing Japanese expatriates with Vietnamese managers.

Trend 3: Greater Demand for Professionals Combining Japanese Language Skills with Expertise

Japanese language proficiency remains an advantage in Japan–Vietnam business environments. However, employers increasingly need to evaluate language skills alongside professional capabilities.

For specialized positions, companies often look for a combination of:

  • Practical professional expertise.

  • Japanese communication and business reporting skills.

  • The ability to understand requirements from Japanese clients or headquarters.

  • Collaboration, problem-solving, and project management capabilities.

For example, a BrSE does more than translate requirements between Japanese clients and Vietnamese software development teams. The role may also involve clarifying business requirements and supporting project delivery.

Similarly, a Japanese-speaking B2B sales professional needs to develop clients, prepare business proposals, and maintain corporate relationships. An IT Consultant must understand client challenges and recommend appropriate solutions.

Therefore, a candidate's value increasingly lies in the combination of professional expertise, Japanese language proficiency, and the ability to work effectively across Japanese and Vietnamese business environments.

Trend 4: Digital Transformation Is Reshaping Talent Requirements

Digital transformation, automation, and data-driven management are creating new workforce requirements across manufacturing, services, and corporate operations.

Beyond traditional IT positions, companies may need system implementation specialists, technology project managers, solution consultants, data analysts, and professionals who can bridge business functions and technical teams.

For Japanese companies, technology recruitment is not simply about software development. Many roles also require solutions to align with business processes, quality standards, and Japanese client expectations.

This makes positions such as BrSE, IT Project Manager, IT Consultant, and Technical Pre-sales Specialist particularly relevant to Japan–Vietnam workforce development.

3. How Should Companies Adapt Their Recruitment Strategies?

As workforce requirements evolve and competition for talent intensifies, companies should look beyond increasing the number of candidate profiles. Recruitment strategies need to be aligned with business objectives.

First, define the required competencies clearly. In addition to qualifications and years of experience, employers should assess practical capabilities, role suitability, and experience working with Japanese companies.

Second, broaden access to suitable candidates. Rather than relying exclusively on active job seekers, companies can approach experienced professionals who may be open to new career opportunities.

Third, evaluate Japanese language skills in context. Employers should distinguish general conversational ability from the ability to use Japanese in professional situations, such as progress reporting, technical discussions, and business negotiations.

Fourth, prioritize employee retention. Career development opportunities, role responsibilities, management practices, and promotion pathways can all influence a candidate's decision to join and remain with a company.

Fifth, work with specialized recruitment partners when necessary. For hard-to-fill positions such as middle management, IT specialists, and Japanese-speaking professionals, a recruitment partner with relevant expertise and candidate networks can help streamline sourcing, screening, and interview coordination.
