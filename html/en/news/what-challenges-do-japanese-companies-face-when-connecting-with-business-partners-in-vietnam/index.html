What Challenges Do Japanese Companies Face When Connecting with Business Partners in Vietnam?

 Economic ties between Japan and Vietnam continue to deepen. For Japanese companies, Vietnam is becoming increasingly important not only as a manufacturing base, but also as a sourcing destination, consumer market, investment destination, and location for developing new business partnerships.

According to Vietnam's Ministry of Industry and Trade, bilateral trade between Vietnam and Japan exceeded USD 51.43 billion in 2025, an increase of 11.28% compared with 2024. Vietnam's exports to Japan reached approximately USD 26.77 billion, while imports from Japan amounted to approximately USD 24.68 billion.

As of January 31, 2026, Japan also had 5,722 active investment projects in Vietnam, with total registered capital of approximately USD 78.9 billion.

These figures demonstrate the significant potential for business cooperation between the two countries. However, in practice, many Japanese companies—particularly small and medium-sized enterprises—continue to face challenges when searching for suppliers, customers, distributors, and business partners in Vietnam.

1. Finding the Right Vietnamese Business Partner

One of the first challenges is identifying Vietnamese companies that can genuinely meet the requirements of Japanese businesses.

Vietnam has a large and growing supplier base. However, information available through websites and online platforms is often insufficient to accurately evaluate a company's production capacity, factory scale, financial capability, quality management system, export experience, and ability to meet delivery requirements.

According to JETRO's 2025 survey, among local procurement by Japanese manufacturers in Vietnam, 48.1% was sourced from Vietnamese companies, compared with 42.8% in the previous year's survey.

This indicates that the role of Vietnamese suppliers is expanding. At the same time, it highlights the increasing importance of identifying, screening, and evaluating potential suppliers, rather than simply compiling a list of companies.

2. Differences in Quality Standards and Business Requirements

Japanese companies generally place strong emphasis on product quality, consistency, delivery performance, traceability, process control, and corrective action when problems occur.

A Vietnamese supplier may be competitive in terms of price and production capacity but may not necessarily meet all the specific standards and management requirements expected by a Japanese customer.

Potential gaps may arise in areas such as technical specifications, consistency between production batches, raw-material management, documentation, traceability, delivery schedules, and handling of defects or customer complaints.

Therefore, the objective is not simply to find a supplier, but to identify a supplier that can consistently meet the quality and operational requirements of Japanese companies.

3. Language and Business Communication Barriers

Language remains another important challenge.

Not every Vietnamese company has employees capable of conducting negotiations in Japanese. Likewise, Japanese SMEs that have not yet established a local subsidiary in Vietnam may lack Vietnamese-speaking employees or personnel with sufficient knowledge of the local market. However, effective business communication involves more than translation.

Successful negotiations require an understanding of the intentions behind questions, quality expectations, delivery requirements, responsibilities, commitments, and decision-making processes.

For this reason, an intermediary that understands both the language and business culture of Japan and Vietnam can play an important role in facilitating communication between the two sides.

4. Verifying the Reliability and Actual Capabilities of Potential Partners

Finding a company online does not necessarily mean finding a company that is ready and capable of doing business.

Before starting a new relationship, Japanese companies often need to verify whether the company is genuinely operating, who the actual decision-maker is, what its production capacity is, whether it has export experience, what certifications it holds, and whether it has the ability to fulfil contractual obligations.

For Japanese companies without their own personnel or networks in Vietnam, conducting this verification process can require significant time and resources.

This illustrates the important difference between “finding company information” and “finding a reliable business partner.”

5. Differences in Decision-Making Speed and Processes

Japanese companies often follow a structured decision-making process before entering into a new business relationship.

This may involve information gathering, internal reporting, risk assessment, supplier comparison, sample testing, negotiations, and internal approval.

Vietnamese companies, on the other hand, may sometimes expect decisions to be made more quickly after several initial discussions.

This difference can create misunderstandings. Vietnamese companies may perceive Japanese companies as taking too long to make decisions, while Japanese companies may feel that their Vietnamese counterparts are pushing for a transaction before sufficient information has been collected.

A clearly managed process—search → screening → initial contact → needs assessment → quotation → sample evaluation → negotiation → contract → follow-up—can therefore help both sides work more effectively.

6. Increasing Competition in the Vietnamese Market

Japanese companies in Vietnam are competing not only with local businesses but also with companies from China, South Korea, Europe, and other markets.

JETRO has noted the increasing expansion of Chinese companies in markets such as Vietnam, contributing to a more competitive business environment.

As a result, Japanese companies seeking to develop the Vietnamese market need more than a list of potential customers.

They need to understand who their target customers are, who the decision-makers are, what competitors are offering, and where their own competitive advantages lie.

7. Growing Demand for Supply Chain Localization

Another significant trend is the increasing interest among Japanese companies in expanding local procurement in Vietnam.

JETRO materials based on its 2024 survey indicated that 50.9% of Japanese-affiliated companies planned to increase local procurement.

In JETRO's 2025 survey, Vietnamese companies accounted for 48.1% of local procurement by Japanese manufacturers surveyed in Vietnam.

This creates significant opportunities for Vietnamese companies seeking to participate more deeply in Japanese supply chains.

However, converting sourcing demand into actual transactions still requires both sides to address challenges related to information, quality standards, communication, supplier verification, and trust.
