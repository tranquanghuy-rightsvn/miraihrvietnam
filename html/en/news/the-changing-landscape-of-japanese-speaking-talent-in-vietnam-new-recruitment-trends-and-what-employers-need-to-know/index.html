The Changing Landscape of Japanese-Speaking Talent in Vietnam: New Recruitment Trends and What Employers Need to Know

1. The Japanese-Speaking Talent Market in Vietnam Is Changing

For many years, Japanese language proficiency has been an important advantage for Vietnamese professionals seeking career opportunities with Japanese companies. Traditionally, recruitment demand has focused on interpreters, translators, assistants, administrative staff, sales personnel, and other roles supporting communication between Japanese companies and their Vietnamese teams.

However, as digital transformation accelerates, foreign investment expands, and cross-border business operations become increasingly sophisticated, the composition of Japan-related talent in Vietnam is undergoing a significant shift.

Employers are increasingly looking for candidates who can do more than communicate in Japanese. They need professionals with specialized expertise, practical experience, problem-solving capabilities, and the ability to collaborate effectively across business functions.

In other words, the value of Japanese-speaking talent is increasingly assessed through a broader combination of capabilities: Japanese language proficiency, professional expertise, and the ability to deliver measurable business results.

This trend has important implications for Japanese companies, foreign-invested enterprises (FDI), and Vietnamese professionals planning their recruitment strategies and career development.

2. What the Data Tells Us About Recruitment Challenges in Vietnam

According to the Japan External Trade Organization (JETRO), its FY2025 survey on the business conditions of Japanese-affiliated companies overseas received responses from 906 Japanese-affiliated companies in Vietnam. The survey was conducted in August–September 2025.

The report highlights several recruitment-related indicators:

  • 48.2%: The reported proportion of companies experiencing greater recruitment difficulties over the preceding two years, according to JETRO's analysis of the recruitment environment.

  • 66.7%: The proportion of Japanese-affiliated manufacturing companies in Vietnam reporting recruitment difficulties.

  • 32.8%: The corresponding proportion among non-manufacturing companies.

  • 76.0%: The proportion of manufacturing companies in northern Vietnam reporting recruitment difficulties, compared with 56.7% in southern Vietnam.

Note: These figures concern recruitment challenges in general among Japanese-affiliated companies in Vietnam. They do not represent statistics specifically for Japanese-speaking professionals.

These figures highlight the growing importance of effective talent acquisition, particularly in manufacturing. For positions requiring both Japanese language proficiency and specialized expertise, companies need clear hiring criteria and access to relevant candidate pools rather than relying solely on language qualifications.

3. Four Key Trends Reshaping Japanese-Speaking Talent in Vietnam

3.1. From Japanese Language Skills to Japanese Plus Professional Expertise

One of the most important changes is how employers evaluate candidates.

In the past, many positions placed considerable emphasis on Japanese communication skills, JLPT N2 or N1 qualifications, and the ability to support communication between Japanese and Vietnamese colleagues. Today, specialized roles increasingly require candidates to demonstrate relevant technical knowledge, practical experience, and the ability to work independently.

Representative talent categories include:

  • IT and technology: Bridge System Engineers (BrSEs), IT communicators, software engineers, IT business analysts, and IT consultants.

  • Enterprise systems: SAP consultants, SAP PP/MM/SD specialists, ERP consultants, and enterprise system implementation professionals.

  • Project management: Project managers, PMO specialists, Scrum Masters, and project coordinators working with Japanese clients.

  • Business development: Business development managers, sales managers, account managers, and Japan–Vietnam market development professionals.

  • Supply chain and logistics: Supply chain management professionals, logistics managers, procurement specialists, and operations managers.

  • Finance and corporate management: Finance and accounting professionals, internal control specialists, and management consultants working with Japanese businesses.

In these roles, Japanese serves as a tool for communicating with clients, business partners, or headquarters in Japan. Professional expertise remains essential to successful job performance.

3.2. Greater Emphasis on Mid-Level and Senior Professionals

Recruitment demand is also extending beyond execution-oriented positions to roles involving management, coordination, and decision-making.

For example, a BrSE may need to understand business requirements, analyze technical specifications, manage project schedules, and communicate directly with Japanese clients.

A project manager may need experience in scope management, budgeting, resource allocation, risk management, and delivery quality.

Similarly, a Japanese-speaking business development manager is expected to identify potential customers, develop partnerships, negotiate commercial terms, and generate revenue—not merely interpret during business meetings.

For these positions, language ability is one component of a broader professional skill set.

3.3. Japanese Combined with English and International Working Skills

Japanese is not necessarily the only working language in companies operating across multiple countries.

In IT, technology consulting, finance, and project management, professionals may use Japanese to communicate with clients or headquarters while using English for technical documentation, enterprise systems, and collaboration with international teams.

Candidates with the following combination may therefore have a competitive advantage:

  1. Japanese proficiency appropriate to the job requirements.

  2. Relevant professional or technical expertise.

  3. Strong communication, collaboration, and cross-cultural working skills.

This does not mean that every position requires English or JLPT N1. Requirements should reflect the actual responsibilities, client expectations, seniority, and working environment of each role.

3.4. A Shift from Qualifications-Based Hiring to Competency-Based Assessment

JLPT N1 and N2 remain useful indicators of Japanese language proficiency. However, a language certificate alone cannot fully demonstrate a candidate's ability to perform effectively in a business environment.

For example, an N1-qualified candidate without project management experience may not meet the requirements of a project manager position requiring several years of relevant experience. Conversely, an N2-qualified candidate with hands-on BrSE experience, software development knowledge, and effective communication skills may be a stronger match for certain technical roles.

Recruitment processes should therefore consider:

  • Japanese proficiency and practical workplace communication.

  • Industry knowledge and role-specific experience.

  • Previous achievements and measurable work outcomes.

  • Problem-solving, teamwork, and task management capabilities.

  • Alignment with company culture and client expectations.

Assessing practical competencies is particularly important when recruiting mid-level and senior professionals.

4. Recruitment Challenges Facing Japanese Companies

Changes in talent requirements create several challenges for employers.

First, finding candidates who meet multiple requirements simultaneously. Candidates with strong Japanese skills may lack the necessary professional expertise, while technically capable candidates may not yet meet Japanese communication requirements.

Second, competition for experienced professionals. Candidates with experience in Japanese business environments, specialized expertise, and the ability to contribute immediately are often attractive to multiple employers.

Third, unclear or overly restrictive hiring criteria. Requiring every candidate to have JLPT N1, extensive experience, multiple certifications, and a relatively low salary expectation can significantly narrow the available talent pool.

Fourth, employee retention. Compensation, career development opportunities, training, management practices, and the working environment can all influence an employee's decision to remain with a company.

To address these challenges, employers should align recruitment criteria with actual job responsibilities and balance existing candidate capabilities with opportunities for training and development after hiring.

5. Five Strategies for Recruiting Japanese-Speaking Professionals

Companies can consider five practical strategies to adapt to the changing market.

1. Define the core competencies of each position

Distinguish essential requirements from preferred qualifications. For an SAP consultant, for example, implementation experience and knowledge of business processes may be more important than meeting a particular JLPT level alone.

2. Expand candidate sourcing channels

Look beyond active job seekers. Potential candidates may already be working for Japanese companies, IT businesses, consulting firms, or organizations serving Japanese clients.

3. Assess candidates across multiple dimensions

Combine technical interviews, job-related scenarios, practical Japanese language assessments, and experience verification to improve hiring decisions.

4. Develop competitive compensation and benefits

Evaluate compensation in relation to market conditions, job responsibilities, professional experience, specialized skills, and the scarcity of relevant talent.

5. Partner with a professional recruitment firm

For specialized or difficult-to-fill positions requiring Japanese, a recruitment partner can help identify candidate sources, screen applications, assess job fit, and reduce the time required to find suitable professionals.
